## 0. Read this first
- MAIN FACT: CONFIRMED from the American Veterinary Medical Association today. "Plain, boneless, and skinless turkey is generally safe for pets in small portions, but other table scraps—including gravy, sauces, dressing, and meat or poultry fat or skin—can be hard for pets to digest, and sometimes even a small amount can cause pancreatitis. The bones themselves can cause choking or intestinal blockage." [fetch]
- SECOND FACT: CONFIRMED from the ASPCA (Thanksgiving Safety Tips, ASPCA Poison Control). If you feed a pet "a small bite of turkey or other meat, make sure it's boneless and fully cooked. Raw or undercooked turkey may contain salmonella bacteria and poultry bones can be a major choking hazard." The same page also says onions and garlic "can be toxic to pets," and it first recommends sticking to the pet's normal diet. [fetch]
- Independent pages opened: ASPCA "Sharing is Caring" (lean cooked turkey free of bones and seasoning; snacks no more than five percent of daily calories; too much fat or sugar can lead to pancreatitis); ASPCA People Foods to Avoid (onions, garlic and chives can damage red blood cells; cats are more susceptible; raw meat may contain Salmonella and E. coli; bones can injure or obstruct the GI tract); FDA Center for Veterinary Medicine holiday-hazards update (do not give high-fat scraps such as skin from a roasted turkey; rich foods can cause upset stomach and pancreatitis; pancreatitis signs in cats are "less clear"); AVMA policy discouraging raw or undercooked animal-sourced protein, including poultry, for dogs and cats; AVMA household-hazards brochure (cooked turkey bones can splinter). All opened 2026-11-15.
- Hedge the pet science. These pages speak of "pets" or "dogs and cats," use "generally," "may," "can," and "sometimes even a small amount." Do not turn that into "your cat will get pancreatitis" or "a little turkey is always fine." Do not invent a serving size (no "one-inch cube") that is not on these pages. The ASPCA's five-percent snack figure is that organization's guideline, not a lab result about every cat.
- Safety lines must carry their condition: bones; seasoning (onions, garlic, chives; also salt / bouillon); skin and fat; cooked versus raw or undercooked.
- The sources DISAGREE, or at least pull in two directions, on gravy: the ASPCA Thanksgiving page offers "a drizzle of gravy" in a homemade pet feast, and on the same page warns that bouillon cubes used for stocks and gravy are high in sodium and can lead to gastrointestinal problems and even seizures. The AVMA Thanksgiving page groups gravy with fat and skin as scraps that can be hard to digest and can sometimes cause pancreatitis. Do not say "gravy is safe."
- Not found on these agency pages: a required serving size in ounces or cubes; a claim that dark meat is safe or unsafe as a class; a bite-force or nutrition-comparison claim; a promise that cooked turkey replaces a complete cat food.

## 2. Three hook facts
1. CT-F9 + CT-F1: Plain, boneless, skinless, fully cooked turkey is generally described as safe for pets in a small portion, and both the AVMA and the ASPCA put conditions on it. The ASPCA first recommends the normal diet.
2. CT-F2 + CT-F9 + CT-F11: The leftover is not safe just because it is turkey — bones can choke, block, splinter or damage the digestive tract; fat and skin can be hard to digest and can sometimes lead to pancreatitis.
3. CT-F3 + CT-F7 + CT-F12: Seasoning matters (onions, garlic and chives can damage red blood cells; cats are more susceptible), and raw or undercooked turkey is discouraged (salmonella and other pathogens).

## 4. Shortened-claim traps
- "Cats can eat turkey" without "plain, boneless, skinless, fully cooked, small amount."
- "Boneless turkey is fine" without "fully cooked" and "unseasoned / no skin or fat."
- "No bones" said as if that were the only hazard.
- "Onions are toxic" without "garlic and chives" if the line is about seasoning in general, and without "can" / "cats are more susceptible."
- "Raw turkey has salmonella" as a certainty; the ASPCA says "may contain."
- "Pancreatitis" without "can" / "sometimes even a small amount" and without naming fat, skin or rich scraps.
- Reciting FDA's dog pancreatitis symptoms as cat symptoms.
- Using the FDA carcass-in-the-trash line as if it were written about cats; the page says "Dogs, especially."
