## UNV-C1 — Why cats scratch (marking, stretching, claw upkeep). **PASS.**

The slate's three reasons are all on live pages. Do not merge the pages into one untagged sentence. The cleanest single-source line that already covers claw upkeep, stretching / exercise, and marking is iCatCare's opening sentence.

**Recommended line (iCatCare, word for word):**

> "Cats scratch to maintain healthy claws, exercise the muscles in their front legs and spine, and as a form of territorial communication or marking behaviour."

Do not say scratching is "revenge" or that it can be stopped. iCatCare: "scratching is a normal and necessary behaviour for cats, so you will not be able to stop it (nor should you – cats need to scratch!)."

### iCatCare — https://icatcare.org/articles/scratching-on-furniture-and-carpets

HTTP 200. "First published 19th Mar 2025." "**Updated 7th Oct 2026.**"

> "Cats scratch to maintain healthy claws, exercise the muscles in their front legs and spine, and as a form of territorial communication or marking behaviour."

Also, on claw upkeep: "Stropping, where cats drag their front claws downwards, either on a horizontal or vertical surface, loosens and removes the outer keratin husk of their claws, revealing sharp new surfaces underneath."

On marking: "Along with urine marking and face rubbing, scratching is used as a marking behaviour for communication with other cats. Glands in between your cat’s pads mix to produce a chemical message, a combination of pheromones and odours."

### Cornell Feline Health Center — https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/feline-behavior-problems-destructive-behavior

HTTP 200. "**Updated March 2018.**"

> "Scratching is largely a marking behavior that deposits scent from special glands on the cat’s paws into his territory and removes the translucent covering, or sheath, from the claws. The scratch marks and claw sheaths left behind may also be displays of confidence."

Cornell supports **marking** and **claw-sheath removal**. It does not, in this scratching section, independently name "stretching" as a reason (it only uses "stretched" when asking whether the cat scratches vertically "with her paws stretched out above her head"). Do not attribute a stretching reason to Cornell.

### AAFP — 2017 Declawing Position Statement, as reproduced in the Claw Friendly Educational Toolkit and on PMC

Live pages opened this session:

- https://catvets.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/AAFPClawFriendlyEducationalToolkit-updated.pdf (HTTP 200)
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11128892/ (HTTP 200)

> "It is important that owners understand that scratching is a normal feline behavior; it is both inherited and learned. The primary reason for scratching is to maintain the necessary claw motion used in hunting and climbing. In addition, it is done to re-establish claw sharpness via ‘husk’ (or ‘sheath’) removal and to stretch the body. Finally, it is an important means of visual and olfactory communication."

Keep AAFP's order and the hedge **"primary reason"** if that sentence is used: AAFP's primary reason is the claw motion used in hunting and climbing, *then* sheath removal and stretching, *then* communication. Do not rearrange this into "the main reason is marking."

## House Guests #1 (EP 5)

### UNV-B1 — Do spiders in tubs come up the drain? **CUT as a fact.** Optional **FIX** as a myth-bust.

The twist "it came up the drain" is not supported as a fact. Two live sources treat it as a myth, or as something spiders were not shown to do. The sourced "why it's here" line is that house spiders walk in looking for water and then cannot climb the porcelain.

**Recommended:** Cut the drain-origin as a factual twist. If the beat needs a twist, bust the myth instead:

> "It didn't come up the drain. The Burke Museum says it doesn't know of even one case of a spider migrating into a house through plumbing, and that modern drains contain a liquid-filled sediment trap through which spiders cannot penetrate. House spiders that reach a tub are usually looking for water and then can't climb back out."

Keep the WSU water-and-stuck wording only with the species and range in UNV-B2.

### Burke Museum (University of Washington), Rod Crawford, Spider Myths

Two URLs, same fact text, both HTTP 200 this session:

- https://www.burkemuseum.org/static/spidermyth/myths/bathtub.html — "This page last updated 1 September, 2010." Text © 2003.
- https://www.burkemuseum.org/collections-and-research/biology/arachnology-and-entomology/spider-myths/myth-spiders-come-through — current path, same wording.

> "Myth: Spiders found in bathtubs or sinks have come up through the drains from the sewers."

> "I don't know of even one case where a spider was actually shown to migrate into a house through plumbing; modern drains contain a liquid-filled sediment trap through which spiders cannot penetrate. House spiders are thirsty creatures living in a very water-poor environment, and any that venture near a sink or tub with drops of water in it will try to reach the water, often by climbing down a wall. Once in the slick-sided porcelain basin, they are unable to climb back out unless a helpful human \"lends them a hand.\""

Keep "I don't know of even one case" and "cannot penetrate." Do not upgrade this to "never, in every drain on earth."

### WSU Pestsense — giant house spider (*Eratigena atrica*)

https://pestsense.cahnrs.wsu.edu/fact-sheet/giant-house-spider/ — HTTP 200. No article date shown on the page this session.

> "The giant house spider is not an extremely active climber and indoors is usually found on the floor or trapped in sinks or bathtubs where it has ventured in search of water."

An Iowa State Extension "Bugs in the Bathroom" page was a lead (https://www.extension.iastate.edu/news/bugs-bathroom) but returned HTTP 403 this session and was **not** used.

### UNV-B2 — Is "probably came for water" fair outside WSU's species and range? **FIX.**

It is not fair as an unscoped line about any spider in any tub. WSU's "ventured in search of water" sentence is about the giant house spider, *Eratigena atrica*, in and around Washington homes west of the Cascades, plus parts of coastal Canada and Oregon, and not east of the Cascades in Washington.

The slate is already right that the PNW giant-house-spider card is required. Research confirms that hedge.

**Do not use:** "That spider in your tub? It probably came for water and got stuck."

**Recommended S1 hook (scoped, WSU wording kept):**

> "In the Pacific Northwest, WSU says the giant house spider is usually 'trapped in sinks or bathtubs where it has ventured in search of water.'"

Card scope, from the same page (keep the page typo "formally" only inside a direct quote; in paraphrase say "formerly"):

> "The giant house spider, Eratigena atrica (formally known as Tegenaria gigantea and T. duellica), is commonly found in and around Washington homes west of the Cascade Mountains. Its range also includes parts of coastal Canada and Oregon. They are not found east of the Cascades in Washington."

Burke's more general "house spiders are thirsty creatures…" line may be used for a broad myth-bust (UNV-B1), but do not turn it into a "probably" rule for every species.

### S2 — "Big brown spider, so it's a hobo? WSU says that's a common mix-up." **FIX** the wording.

The mix-up is real on the WSU giant-house-spider page, but the proposed hook widens it. WSU does not say that any big brown spider is commonly a hobo mix-up, and it does not use the words "common mix-up." It says **giant house spiders are frequently mistaken for hobo spiders**, and that **color, size, and markings are not reliable** ID features for this spider. Smaller males are often the ones mistaken, and the Puget Sound comparison stays in Puget Sound.

**Recommended S2 hook:**

> "Big brown spider, so it's a hobo? WSU says giant house spiders are frequently mistaken for hobo spiders. Because color, size, and markings vary, none of them is a reliable way to identify this spider."

Live quotes, same WSU page as above (HTTP 200):

> "NOTE: Giant house spiders are frequently mistaken for hobo spiders."

> "The smaller males are often mistaken for hobo spiders; however, in the Puget Sound region giant house spiders outnumber hobo spiders and are much more frequently encountered."

> "Because of the variability in color, size, and markings, none of these are reliable features for identifying this spider."

Keep "frequently mistaken," "often mistaken" for the smaller males, and the Puget Sound clause if that comparison is used. Do not drop the species name or move this mix-up nationwide.

## Art ID-check: `/workspace/animation/bugs-concept-v1.jpg`

The image was viewed this session. Social Studio's concern was that the labeled **"HOUSE SPIDER"** looks like a wolf spider or tarantula, while common house spiders (*Parasteatoda*, e.g. *P. tepidariorum*) are small and round-bodied. That concern is **confirmed**. No Animator sidecar notes file for this image was found in `/workspace/animation/`.

Geometric drawing, cartoon eyes, omitted mouthparts, and not-to-scale are treated as acceptable cartoon choices unless they mislead an ID, per the request.

### HOUSE SPIDER — **FIX** (wrong silhouette / wrong species cues)

What the drawing shows: a stocky, robust, bright golden spider with thick legs that end in black tips, a large cephalothorax, and a bold dark heart- or chevron-shaped badge on a pale, spotted abdomen. That is a hunting-spider / wolf-spider / tarantula proportion, not a small cobweb weaver.

It also does not match the Pacific Northwest **giant house spider** (*Eratigena atrica*) used in House Guests #1, which WSU describes as brown to grayish-brown and "somewhat hairy and … not shiny."

**If the label is meant to be the common / American house spider (*Parasteatoda tepidariorum*), change:**

1. Shrink it next to the stink bug and house centipede. This is a small cobweb spider, not a large hunter.
2. Make the abdomen globular and higher than it is long, in drab dirty-white / tan / brown with gray chevrons or V-shapes, and a pale patch behind the high point of the abdomen.
3. Slim the legs. Color them yellow to yellowish-brown with darker rings at the end of each segment, not thick black-tipped tarantula legs.
4. Use a yellowish-brown carapace, not a bright gold "cute hunting spider" face.
5. Optional but helpful: a messy, three-dimensional cobweb in a corner, which is how this species is found.
6. Relabel "COMMON HOUSE SPIDER" or "AMERICAN HOUSE SPIDER" so it is not confused with the PNW giant house spider.

**If the Animator instead meant the PNW giant house spider for the bathtub episode:** relabel it **"GIANT HOUSE SPIDER,"** paint it brown to grayish-brown and somewhat hairy (not shiny gold), and give it a longer-legged funnel-weaver silhouette. Still do not draw a wolf spider. Wolf spiders do not build a capture web.

**Source quotes (live this session):**

Penn State Extension, Common House Spider, https://extension.psu.edu/common-house-spider — HTTP 200, **updated January 19, 2026.** *Parasteatoda tepidariorum*, family Theridiidae (cobweb weavers):

> "The common house spider female is 5 to 6 millimeters in length with a yellowish-brown carapace and a dirty-white to brown abdomen with gray chevrons. The legs are yellow, with darker rings at the end of each segment. Some individuals have a triangular black spot on the top of the abdomen. The male is 3.8 to 4.7 millimeters in length with orange legs."

Missouri Department of Conservation, Common House Spider, https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/common-house-spider — HTTP 200. No article date shown this session.

> "The overall color is drab: yellowish, tan, brown, or gray, with darker mottling or streaks. The abdomen is round, higher than long, usually with streaks on the side and V-shapes behind. There is usually a whitish patch just behind the highest point of the abdomen. The legs are usually ringed with a dark color."

> "Size Length (not including legs): ⅛ to ⅜ inch (females); ⅛ inch (males)"

Colorado State University fact sheet, American House Spider (*Parasteatoda tepidariorum*), https://webdoc.agsci.colostate.edu/bspm/InsectInformation/FactSheets/AHouseSpider.pdf — HTTP 200.

> "The abdomen is generally round, somewhat higher than long with a light spot at the tip. Overall color is gray or light brown with black and white markings. The cephalothorax is yellow to brown and the legs are yellow to brown with darker rings. Males (ca 4mm body length) are smaller and darker than the females (5-8mm body length)."

Wolf-spider contrast, Missouri Department of Conservation, https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/wolf-spiders — HTTP 200:

> "These are athletic spiders that don’t spin webs to catch their prey; instead, they run it down. They have long legs and are usually gray, brown, black, or tan with dark brown or black body markings (especially stripes)."

> "Similar species: Tarantulas are much larger and heavier."

> "Length (not including legs): from ¼ inch to 1 inch (varies by species; males are usually smaller than females)."

Giant house spider contrast, WSU (URL above):

> "These brown to grayish-brown spiders are highly variable in size and color."

> "The legs and body of the giant house spider are somewhat hairy and are not shiny in appearance."

> "The body is typically around 1/2 inch to 1 inch in length (not including the legs); while the leg span for large adults can reach up to 4 inches."

### CELLAR SPIDER — **PASS**

Small body, very long thin legs, a distinct cephalothorax plus a longer abdomen. That reads as a pholcid (cellar / daddy-long-legs spider), not a harvestman. Cartoon eyes are acceptable. No art fix required.

### STINK BUG — **PASS**

Shield-shaped, mottled brown body reads as a brown marmorated stink bug cartoon. Simplified antennae, cartoon eyes, and not-to-scale are acceptable and do not mislead the shield-bug ID. No art fix required.

### HOUSE CENTIPEDE — **PASS** as a cartoon, with optional ID improvements

Banded legs, many legs, long antennae, and a long hind pair already cue *Scutigera coleoptrata*. Geometric segments, cartoon eyes, omitted mouthparts, and not-to-scale are acceptable. The drawing is not a millipede or a soil centipede.

Optional, only if the Animator wants a tighter ID (these are the features the extension pages actually lead with):

- Add **three dark longitudinal stripes** on a dirty-yellow / grayish-yellow back.
- Lengthen the last pair of legs (Penn State: on adult females, "nearly twice the length of the body") and the antennae (WSU: antennae and last pair of legs are both longer than the body).
- Keep the body slender and flattened, not thick.

**Source quotes:**

WSU, https://pestsense.cahnrs.wsu.edu/fact-sheet/house-centipede/ — HTTP 200. No article date shown this session.

> "The house centipede (Scutigera coleoptrata) is a slender, flattened, many-segmented arthropod approximately 1 to 1 1/2 inches long."

> "This centipede is grayish-yellow in color with three dark stripes running along its back. Adults have fifteen pairs of long, fragile legs. The long, delicate antennae and the last pair of legs are both longer than the body."

Penn State Extension, House Centipedes, https://extension.psu.edu/house-centipedes — HTTP 200, **updated January 19, 2026.**

> "Adult house centipedes have 15 pairs of legs with the last pair (on adult females) nearly twice the length of the body, which is one to one and one-half inches in length … This gives the centipede an overall appearance of being from three to four inches in length (including legs and antennae). The legs are banded light and dark, and the body is a dirty yellow with three longitudinal, dark stripes."

WSU also notes: "As with all centipedes, house centipedes have strong mouthparts with large jaws. They may inflict a painful bite if handled." Omitted mouthparts are an allowed cartoon choice here and do not, by themselves, make this the wrong animal. Do not add a scary jaw close-up unless the script is actually about the bite, and then keep "may" and "if handled."

### SILVERFISH — **FIX** (add the third tail filament)

The silver, tapered, fish-like body and long antennae are fine. The art appears to show only **two** posterior bristles. Silverfish and firebrats are identified by **three** long tail-like appendages (two cerci plus a medial filament). Two tails can mislead an ID. Cartoon eyes are fine.

**Fix:** Add a third, central tail filament, about as long as the two side ones.

Ohio State University Extension, https://ohioline.osu.edu/factsheet/ENT-64 — HTTP 200, **date 05/13/2015.**

> "These wingless insects have two slender antennae, nearly as long as the body, and three long, antenna-like appendages (two cerci and a medial filament) at the tip of their abdomens."

University of Maryland Extension, https://extension.umd.edu/resource/silverfish-and-firebrats — HTTP 200.

> "They also have three long, thin, tail-like appendages at the end of the abdomen."

## Unsafe-to-imitate flags

- Do not tell people to handle an unidentified spider, or a widow or recluse, with bare hands.
- Do not recommend spraying a tub spider as the default. UC IPM: pesticides are not generally needed. WSU: leave giant house spiders alone as much as reasonably possible.
- If bitten: WSU's line is expert identification and "you may wish to seek medical attention," not a guarantee and not a DIY venom test.
- Cats: do not recommend punishment for scratching (out of scope for UNV-C1, but already on the iCatCare and Cornell pages). Do not present declawing as a casual tip.
