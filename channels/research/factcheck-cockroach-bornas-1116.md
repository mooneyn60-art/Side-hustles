## Special checks

### 1. Hook: "Your kitchen: one German female's family can top 30,000 a year."

**Pass, with a wording note on "top."** Do not use a "one female" line that drops the offspring.

Live UC IPM (CR22): "This species has the fastest reproductive cycle of all the common pest cockroaches: a single female and her offspring can produce over 30,000 individuals in a year." URL: https://ipm.ucanr.edu/home-and-landscape/cockroaches/

The pack's must-travel conditions are: German; "a single female and her offspring"; "over 30,000"; "in a year"; "can."

- **"Family"** fairly stands in for "a single female and her offspring." It is not "one female" alone (M4). The hook card keeps the official chip `A FEMALE AND HER OFFSPRING`. Chapter 4 (s4.l2) and the verdict sweet line (s10.l2) speak the official "a single female and her offspring can produce over thirty thousand individuals in a year."
- **"Top 30,000"** is a looser verb than "produce over 30,000 individuals." In ordinary use "can top 30,000" often means exceed 30,000, but it can also be heard as a ceiling of 30,000, which is weaker than UC IPM's "over." That is not blocking, because the hook vis already chips `OVER 30,000`, and s4.l2 speaks "over." Optional tighter hook, 12 words, at the cap: "Your kitchen: one German female's family can make over 30,000 a year."
- **"Your kitchen," "German," "can," and "a year"** stay. The figure is not hung on American or on "a cockroach."

s10.l4's callback, "One German female's family can top 30,000 a year," is the same wording and gets the same verdict. The long-form description still opens with "a German female and her offspring can make over 30,000," which is closer to the official sentence and is fine as description.

**900× live verification, and a recommended wording fix.** Two live sources, not quite the same sentence:

UC Berkeley News, Robert Sanders, 8 February 2016 (CR15; this is what the script tags and quotes):

> "Roaches traversing crevices, Jayaram found, can withstand forces 900 times their body weight without injury."

URL: https://news.berkeley.edu/2016/02/08/cockroach-inspires-robot-that-squeezes-through-cracks/

The PNAS paper itself (Jayaram and Full, 2016, *PNAS* 113(8): E950–E957, doi 10.1073/pnas.1514591113) was 403 on the publisher site this assignment, as in the pack. It was opened live via PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4776529/. Significance / opening:

> "Using materials tests, we found that the compressive forces cockroaches experience when traversing the smallest crevices were 300 times body weight. Cockroaches withstood forces nearly 900 times body weight without injury, explaining their robustness to compression."

Abstract: "Exoskeletal strength allowed cockroaches to withstand forces 300 times body weight when traversing the smallest crevices and up to nearly 900 times body weight without injury."

Results: "At the smallest crevice or ceiling height of 3.2 mm, the net normal force (2.25 ± 1.17 N) on cockroaches averaged ∼300 times body weight. … At the faster rate of compression, net forces (6.08 ± 1.61 N) attained values of 700 times body weight with a maximum of ∼900 times. We observed no damage to any body part."

The study animal is the American cockroach, *Periplaneta americana*. "Without injury" is the paper's wording (and Berkeley's). The 900× figure is a **maximum** ("nearly 900," "up to nearly 900," "a maximum of ∼900") from **dynamic compressive cycle tests**, at the faster rate. The force **experienced while traversing the smallest crevices** is about **300×** body weight. Berkeley's "roaches traversing crevices … 900 times" collapses those two numbers. The script inherits that collapse.

Corrected s7.l3 (recommended): "In a study of American cockroaches, they withstood forces nearly nine hundred times their body weight without injury." Optional following clause, if a second sentence is wanted: "The forces they experience when traversing the smallest crevices were about three hundred times body weight." Keep the American tag, "nearly" / "up to nearly," "without injury," and no crush or step-on demo. s7.l4 can stay as the American / "without injury" reminder, or say "nearly nine hundred."

Flattened-run and pennies lines (s7.l5–s7.l8) match Berkeley and are consistent with the paper ("run at high speed even when flattened in half"; "traversed horizontal crevices as small as 3 mm, the height of two stacked US pennies"; "if highly motivated" remains the Berkeley condition on the tightest slit). Pass, with American tags.
