# Fact-check: What happens to a spider after you throw it outside?

**Due:** Wed Nov 18, 2026, 5:00 PM ET  
**Written:** Mon Nov 16, 2026, late evening ET (America/New_York)  
**Topic:** What happens to a spider after you throw it outside?

**Rules followed:** University extension, entomology-department, or museum pages only, opened this session. Quotes and hedges are word for word. Gaps are stated plainly. Nothing was messaged or posted.

---

## Bottom line

The Burke Museum / Rod Crawford page **does** address this, and it is the strongest museum source on throwing a *house* spider outdoors. The popular shorthand ("most house spiders can't survive outdoors") is close, but it is **stronger than the page**. Crawford's exact wording is: **"Although some house spider species can survive outdoors, most don't do well there, and some (which are native to other climates) will perish rather quickly when removed from the protective indoor habitat."** The myths index summarizes: **"it probably won't survive there."** Keep those hedges. Do not say it will **definitely** die, or that **all** house spiders die outside.

It also is not true that every spider found indoors is a "house spider," or that releasing one outdoors is always a favor or always a death sentence. Some common indoor species also live around buildings or under boards and bridges. Several extension pages still recommend a jar-and-paper release as a less-toxic way to *remove* a spider from a room. Those are removal instructions, not survival studies. They do not cancel Burke's hedges for true indoor species.

**CUT** as facts: "it will definitely die," "all house spiders die outside," "it'll come right back in."

**Safety:** do not hand-catch or jar a spider that might be a widow or a recluse. Wear gloves around woodpiles and clutter. This is not a treatment or medical guide.

## Does Burke / Rod Crawford really say this?

**Yes.** The live page is:

https://www.burkemuseum.org/collections-and-research/biology/arachnology-and-entomology/spider-myths/myth-house-spiders-belong

Myth (quoted on the page):

> "I'm very kind to spiders; when I find one in the house, I put it back outside instead of killing it."

Fact (full wording, keep every hedge):

> "You can't put something "back" outside that was never outside in the first place. Although some house spider species can survive outdoors, most don't do well there, and some (which are native to other climates) will perish rather quickly when removed from the protective indoor habitat. You're not doing them a favor."

> "In any case, house spiders are mostly harmless and beneficial. … My advice is, "just wave as they go by.""

The Spider Myths index (same site) summarizes that page as:

> "If you put a house spider out in the yard, you aren't doing it any favor; it probably won't survive there."

https://www.burkemuseum.org/collections-and-research/biology/arachnology-and-entomology/spider-myths

Use the **full-page** wording as the lead line. The index's **"probably won't survive"** is a shorter summary and may be used if **"probably"** stays. Do not upgrade either sentence to "can't survive," "will die," or "always dies."

A related Burke page explains *why* "back outside" is the wrong frame: true house spiders are usually not the same species as the yard or garden spiders, and most indoor sightings were never outdoors to begin with.

https://www.burkemuseum.org/collections-and-research/biology/arachnology-and-entomology/spider-myths/myth-spiders-come-indoors

> "In reality, house spiders are usually not the same species as the yard or garden spiders outside the house. House spiders belong to a small number of species specially adapted for indoor conditions (constant climate, poor food supply, very poor water supply). Some house spider species have been living indoors at least since the days of the Roman Empire, and are seldom to be found outside, even in their native countries (usually Europe). Many of these species now live in houses worldwide, and most have been carried by commerce to more than one continent. Few are adapted to North American outdoor environments."

> "House spiders colonize new houses by egg sacs carried on furniture, building materials and so forth. They usually spend their entire life cycle in, on or under their native building."

> "Generally fewer than 5% of the spiders you see indoors have ever been outdoors."

Caption under a diagram on that same page ("Diagram of species of spiders that live indoors vs outdoors"):

> "Only 8 of these 170 Seattle spider species are commonly found both outdoors and in houses."

Keep **"Seattle"** and **"commonly found"** on that 8-of-170 line. It is a Seattle count, not a nationwide rule.

## Claim verdicts

### "Most house spiders can't survive outdoors" / "it will die outside"

**FIX.** Burke is the source, but the popular wording overclaims.

**Recommended line:**  
"Although some house spider species can survive outdoors, most don't do well there, and some (which are native to other climates) will perish rather quickly when removed from the protective indoor habitat. You're not doing them a favor."  
— Burke Museum / Rod Crawford, https://www.burkemuseum.org/collections-and-research/biology/arachnology-and-entomology/spider-myths/myth-house-spiders-belong

Shorter, still hedged: "If you put a house spider out in the yard, you aren't doing it any favor; it probably won't survive there." (myths index)

### "It will definitely die" / "all house spiders die outside"

**CUT.** Burke says **some** species can survive outdoors, **most don't do well**, and **some** (native to other climates) **will perish rather quickly**. That is not "all" and not "definitely."

### "It'll come right back in"

**CUT.** No university, extension, or museum page opened this session says a spider thrown outside will come right back in. Burke says true house spiders "usually spend their entire life cycle in, on or under their native building," which is about origin, not a return trip after being dumped in the yard. Do not invent this.

### "Just put it back outside; you're doing it a favor"

**FIX / CUT as a favor claim** for a true house spider. Burke: "You can't put something "back" outside that was never outside in the first place. … You're not doing them a favor."

Jar-and-release can still be used as a *removal* option for a spider that is not a widow or recluse (see extension lines below). Do not sell it as a kindness that guarantees survival.

## Usable lines, with conditions word for word

**1. Burke, throwing a house spider out (lead).**  
Some can survive outdoors; most don't do well; some from other climates perish rather quickly. You're not doing them a favor. URL above.

**2. Burke, indoor vs outdoor species, climate, and "who you actually found."**  
House spiders are "specially adapted for indoor conditions (constant climate, poor food supply, very poor water supply)." "Few are adapted to North American outdoor environments." "Generally fewer than 5% of the spiders you see indoors have ever been outdoors." Seattle: "Only 8 of these 170 Seattle spider species are commonly found both outdoors and in houses."  
https://www.burkemuseum.org/collections-and-research/biology/arachnology-and-entomology/spider-myths/myth-spiders-come-indoors

**3. Burke, season (do not say they come in "to get out of the cold").**  
Same page:

> "If a large number appear at a specific season, it is usually late summer (August and September)—not a notably cold time of year!—rather than fall, and their appearance coincides with the mating season of the given species. What you are seeing is sexually mature males wandering in search of mates."

> "In contrast, outdoor spider species are not adapted to indoor conditions. Any North American spider that needed artificial shelter for the winter, would have been extinct long before Europeans arrived! Spiders are "cold-blooded" and not attracted to warmth. They don't shiver or get uncomfortable when it's cold, they just become less active and eventually, dormant. Most temperate zone spiders have enough "antifreeze" in their bodies that they won't freeze at any temperature down to -5° C.; some can get colder. The few typical outdoor spiders that do end up indoors, die or at least don't reproduce."

Keep **"-5° C."** and **"Most temperate zone spiders"** if the antifreeze line is used.

**4. Colorado State University Extension — some *other* spiders do wander in during early fall.**  
This is not a contradiction you can flatten into Burke. CSU is talking about some spiders that wander indoors, not about Burke's indoor-adapted house-spider fauna. Quote it as CSU, with its "some" hedge.

https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/spiders-in-the-home/  
*Spiders in the Home,* F.B. Peairs, W.S. Cranshaw, P.E. Cushing; published September 2013, reviewed August 2025.

> "Some spiders wander indoors in the early fall when cooler outdoor temperatures force them to find shelter."

> "Common spiders found indoors include funnel web spiders, cobweb spiders, cellar spiders, and sac spiders."

> "Occasional spiders can be removed by hand (wear gloves or trap the spider in a container) or with a vacuum."

Do not use CSU's fall-shelter line as if it described typical house spiders coming in from the cold, and do not use Burke to say *no* spider ever wanders in.

**5. Extension jar-and-release (removal, not a survival guarantee).**

UC IPM *Spiders*, updated 10/2020, https://ipm.ucanr.edu/home-and-landscape/spiders/:

> "Squash spiders or capture them in a jar and release them outside."

> "Individual spiders can also be removed from indoor areas by placing a jar over them and slipping a piece of paper under the jar that then seals off the opening of the jar when it is lifted up. They can then be released outdoors."

Oregon State University Solve Pest Problems, *Spiders,* updated Apr 03, 2026, https://solvepestproblems.oregonstate.edu/insects-spiders/spiders:

> "Remove spiders found indoors. Leave spiders outdoors alone."

> "If you have spiders indoors, take action to capture them and move them outdoors. Tolerate spiders outdoors, if possible."

These lines do **not** say the spider will live, will die, or will come back in. If a script uses them, keep them as a removal option and do not contradict Burke by promising a favor or a survival.

**6. UC IPM, brown widows (species-specific outdoor hedge).**  
*Widow Spiders and Their Relatives,* updated 03/2017, https://ipm.ucanr.edu/home-and-landscape/widow-spiders-and-their-relatives/

> "If you are concerned about wildlife and feel comfortable doing so, you can remove individual spiders from indoor areas by placing a jar over them and slipping a piece of paper underneath to seal off the opening when you lift the jar. Release the spider about 100 feet from your home into a natural area. Brown widows may not survive in natural areas but on the other hand, they are an invasive, non-native pest species and should not be there anyway."

Keep **"may not survive in natural areas,"** **"about 100 feet,"** and the invasive/non-native clause. This is a brown-widow line, not a rule for every house spider. **Safety:** this is still a jar removal of a widow; do not script bare-handed catching, and do not treat it as casual handling advice.

## Which species are indoor / synanthropic vs. wanderers

"House spider" is not one animal. Do not apply Burke's "most don't do well" line to a wolf spider, an orb weaver, or every brown spider in a tub without saying which kind you mean. Sources also show that some classic indoor species are not strictly indoor-only; they often live *on and around buildings*, not in "the wild" generally.

### *Parasteatoda tepidariorum* (common / American house spider)

**Indoor and also around structures / sheltered outdoor sites.** Not a "dies the moment it hits the grass" fact, and not a wild-countryside spider.

Penn State Extension, *Common House Spider,* updated January 19, 2026 (Steve Jacobs, former Sr. Extension Associate):  
https://extension.psu.edu/common-house-spider

> "Parasteatoda tepidariorum is a cosmopolitan spider that is widely distributed throughout most of the world. It is extremely common in barns and houses, where it constructs webs in the corners of walls, floor joists, and windows. The common house spider may also be found outside under objects such as rocks and boards, as well as beneath bridges and similar structures. In homes, it is most often encountered in damp areas such as basements and crawl spaces."

University of Florida / IFAS, EENY-238 *The Common House Spider, Parasteatoda tepidariorum,* Glavis B. Edwards (PDF fetched from https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/IN394/pdf; doi.org/10.32473/EDIS-in394-2001):

> "Webs may be built both inside and outside of buildings. When inside, they are frequently a major contributor to the build-up of "cobwebs." Sheds, privies, barns, and stables, in addition to dwellings, may have heavy populations of this species. Other characteristic habitats include undersides of highway bridges and culverts. It is largely absent in wild situations except around entrances and in outer chambers of caves, on dry mountain ledges, and on dry ledges of river bluffs (Archer 1947)."

> "At least in Florida, all stages seem to occur throughout the year."

Keep **"largely absent in wild situations except…"** and the Florida season hedge.

### *Pholcus phalangioides* (long-bodied cellar spider)

**Strongly associated with buildings; seldom found in adjacent outdoor gardens** (Portland State). UMN describes it as a home/basement spider.

Portland State University, Susan Masta, *Spiders Commonly Found in Houses,* https://web.pdx.edu/~smasta/MastaSpidersHome.html

> "These spiders can be found most of the year hanging from their webs in corners of dimly lit rooms. They are especially common in cellars (hence the common name "cellar spider"), in stairwells or under stairs, and in other less frequently used areas of buildings. These spiders show a close association with human dwellings, and are seldom found in adjacent outdoor gardens."

University of Minnesota Extension, Jeff Hahn, cellar spider ID page:  
https://apps.extension.umn.edu/garden/diagnose/insect/indoor/relatives/medium/cellar-spider.html

> "Where/When: Found throughout homes in dark areas, especially in basements. Webs are typically in corners. Most common during spring and summer, although can be found throughout the year."

Essig Museum of Entomology, UC Berkeley, *Common synanthropic spiders in California,* https://essig.berkeley.edu/identifications/spiders/

> "The common synanthropic cellar spiders in California are European invasives, native pholcids are much smaller."

No page opened this session says what happens if a cellar spider is thrown onto a lawn. Do not fill that gap. Burke's general house-spider hedges may be used if the script is clearly about an indoor-adapted house spider, not as a *P. phalangioides*-specific death claim.

### *Tegenaria domestica* (barn funnel weaver / domestic house spider) and the *Tegenaria* / *Eratigena* group

Portland State (same Masta page) treats three funnel weavers around Portland residences together: the barn funnel weaver (*Tegenaria domestica*), the giant house spider (*Tegenaria gigantia* [*sic*; this is the *Eratigena atrica* / *T. gigantea* group]), and the hobo spider (*Tegenaria agrestis*):

> "All of these spiders build funnel webs in dark, moist areas such as in woodpiles, under rocks, or in basements. … The spiders that people most commonly encounter in their homes are adult males, wandering in search of females. … Males are most commonly seen during July–September when they wander in search of females."

That puts *T. domestica* in the same "woodpiles, under rocks, or in basements" habitat list as the others, in the Portland area. It is not an indoor-only certificate, and it is not a lawn-survival study.

Essig Museum (California):

> "Their cousins (*Tegenaria* spp., Agelenidae), including the domestic house spider and hobo spider, are more common indoors in some parts of the western states."

Keep **"in some parts of the western states."**

A University of Minnesota / Minnesota Academy of Science paper on Twin Cities synanthropic spiders was requested this session (https://digitalcommons.morris.umn.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1461&context=jmas) and returned **HTTP 403**. It is **not** used.

### *Eratigena atrica* (giant house spider; PNW)

**Synanthropic, ideally adapted to life indoors, but also found outdoors around structures** (firewood, gaps in bricks or stones) in its Pacific Northwest range. Not "never outside," and not a generic U.S. house spider.

Washington State University Pestsense, *Giant house spider,* last review date 2025-04-25:  
https://pestsense.cahnrs.wsu.edu/fact-sheet/giant-house-spider/

> "The giant house spider, Eratigena atrica (formally known as Tegenaria gigantea and T. duellica), is commonly found in and around Washington homes west of the Cascade Mountains. Its range also includes parts of coastal Canada and Oregon. They are not found east of the Cascades in Washington."

> "It is often seen in mid-summer to early fall, when males in particular are highly mobile."

> "Giant house spiders prefer dark areas for web-building and are ideally adapted to life indoors. They are often found in garages or basements, as well as outdoors in firewood or in gaps between bricks or stones."

If a giant house spider is "thrown outside," the sourced outdoor habitat is **firewood or gaps between bricks or stones**, in that range — not a general claim that the yard or the grass will sustain it. No WSU sentence says it dies, or thrives, if tossed onto a lawn. Do not invent one.

Burke on indoor "wolf spiders": large, conspicuous indoor hairy spiders are "most often males of the European House Spider group (genus *Tegenaria*, now sometimes called *Eratigena*)."  
https://www.burkemuseum.org/collections-and-research/biology/arachnology-and-entomology/spider-myths/myth-wolf-spiders-live

### Wanderers (not the true house-spider fauna)

Burke, *Myth: Wolf spiders live indoors:*

> "What's more, in North America no member of the wolf spider family is part of the house spider fauna. … Some very common wolf spiders occasionally wander indoors in some parts of the USA (*Schizocosa* in the southwest, *Tigrosa helluo* in the northeast) but don't survive there."

CSU: some spiders wander indoors in early fall (quoted above).  
UC IPM still lists capture-and-release outside as a general indoor-spider option (quoted above).

A wanderer that already lives outdoors is a different case from a European indoor species. Identify, or hedge. Do not use Burke's "perish rather quickly" line on a wolf spider or a garden orb weaver.

## Regional and seasonal conditions (short)

- **Climate / origin:** Burke: many house spiders are European (or otherwise non-North American outdoor) species carried by commerce; "Few are adapted to North American outdoor environments"; some "which are native to other climates will perish rather quickly" indoors-to-outdoors.
- **Indoor climate:** Burke: house spiders are adapted to "constant climate, poor food supply, very poor water supply."
- **Winter / cold:** Burke: typical outdoor temperate-zone spiders become less active or dormant and, for most, "won't freeze at any temperature down to -5° C."; they did not need houses to survive winter. Typical outdoor species that end up indoors "die or at least don't reproduce."
- **Late-summer indoor flush:** Burke: often August–September mating males, not "coming in from the cold."
- **CSU exception to keep in its own words:** "Some spiders wander indoors in the early fall when cooler outdoor temperatures force them to find shelter."
- **Range examples:** *E. atrica* is a PNW / west-of-Cascades fact (WSU). *P. tepidariorum* is cosmopolitan and, in Florida, can be found in all stages year-round (UF/IFAS). Cellar spiders: close to buildings (PSU faculty / UMN). Do not nationalize a Seattle 8-of-170 count or a Washington range.

## Safety

- **Do not hand-catch, corner, or jar a spider that might be a widow or a recluse.** If a script shows a jar, keep "only if it's not a widow or recluse" and "never with bare hands."
- CSU: "Occasional spiders can be removed by hand **(wear gloves or trap the spider in a container)** or with a vacuum."
- UC IPM widows: "never pick up pieces of wood unless you are wearing gloves." "Be careful that you do not carry spiders indoors on items such as plants, firewood, and boxes." The brown-widow jar line is conditional ("If you are concerned about wildlife and feel comfortable doing so") and is not a dare to handle widows bare-handed.
- Get an unidentified widow or recluse suspect identified. This is not medical advice.

## Recommended sourced package for a Short

Hook (question only): "What happens to a spider after you throw it outside?"

Answer, if it is a true house spider: Burke's "some can survive / most don't do well / some perish rather quickly / you're not doing them a favor." Optional: you can't put it "back" outside if it was never outside; generally fewer than 5% of the spiders you see indoors have ever been outdoors.

Do not say it definitely dies, that every species dies, or that it will come right back in.

If you name a species, use that species' habitat line (for example, PSU's rocks, boards, and bridges for *P. tepidariorum*; WSU's firewood and brick gaps, west of the Cascades, for *E. atrica*).

If the script also shows a jar-and-release, treat it as a removal option for a spider that is not a widow or recluse, not as proof it will live in the yard.
