# Fact pack: "Most House Spiders Can Be Left Alone if You Identify Them 🕷️ (Is This Spider Venomous?)" (house-spider ID long-form)

Prepared for pitch 1 in `/workspace/drafts/longform-pipeline-pitch-1117.md` (multi-creature "don't squish / ID" episode, not a born-as and not a remake of the 11/14 cellar-spider long-form). Research date 2026-11-15 (ET). Row IDs HS1 to HS72. Format follows the crocodile pack: ID | fact | exact quote | URL | scope and how it was opened | do not say.

**Lead species: none for the whole pack.** This episode is explicitly multi-creature. Frame 0 is a longbodied cellar spider (*Pholcus phalangioides*) already wrapping prey, but every number stays tagged to its species, family or study animal. Do not mix *Pholcus phalangioides* with *Physocyclus mexicanus* or *Holocnemus pluchei*; *Loxosceles reclusa* with other recluse species; *Latrodectus* widows with *Steatoda* false widows; *Steatoda grossa* with skull-backed *Steatoda nobilis*; wolf spiders (Lycosidae) with giant house spiders (*Eratigena atrica*); or a Missouri *Colonus puerperus* jump figure with "jumping spiders" in general.

Piles this pack locks (do not invent a title count of "3 and 2"):
- **Leave alone if identified:** longbodied cellar spider (*Pholcus phalangioides*); jumping spiders (Salticidae), with the "40 body lengths" figure only for some species including *Colonus puerperus*; wolf spiders (Lycosidae); giant house spider (*Eratigena atrica*) only inside its Pacific Northwest range.
- **Get identified, do not guess:** black widow and brown widow (*Latrodectus*) versus false widows (*Steatoda*); brown recluse and other recluse spiders (*Loxosceles*) versus lookalikes.

## Hook numbers

Sourced numbers that fit "you + your home + a number or threat," each tied to a row, with the hedge that must travel with the line. These are for packaging and cold-open tests, not unsourced title inventions.

Hedge: one 19th-century house in an endemic area (Lenexa, Kansas, on the UCR page); collected in 6 months; *Loxosceles reclusa* only. Do not put 2,055 on "your house" outside that range. For bites, do not blend the sources: UC IPM says only once in 10 years did anyone show evidence of a bite, a finger that turned red and healed without incident; the UCR author page, citing the same paper, says no person or pet showed evidence of a bite in 8 years as of September 2004 (D1).
2. **A Washington house spider can span 4 inches.** Tied to HS36. Hedge: giant house spider *Eratigena atrica*; leg span, not body length; large adults; west of the Cascades in Washington, plus parts of coastal Canada and Oregon; not found east of the Cascades in Washington. 3. **About 10% of brown recluse bites cause moderate or greater tissue damage.** Tied to HS54 (UCR complement: ninety percent are not medically significant, HS55). Hedge: brown recluse bites, not "spider bites"; "about 10%" / "vast majority only result in inflammation and heal well without medical intervention." This is the official hedge for "most bites don't rot skin." Still seek medical care if a bite is suspected (HS63–HS64). CDC states that venom can cause a severe necrotic lesion that needs professional care (HS56).
4. **Some Missouri jumpers can leap 40 times their length.** Tied to HS24. Hedge: "some species," including *Colonus puerperus*, common in grassy areas throughout Missouri; horizontally; "up to." ## Disagreements

- **D1. The Kansas house, bites.** UC IPM (HS51): 2,055 recluses in 6 months; only once in 10 years did anyone show evidence of a bite (a finger that turned red and healed). UCR author page (HS52): same 2,055, about 450 large enough to envenomate, no person or pet showed evidence of a bite in 8 years as of September 2004, citing the 2002 paper. The paper PDF was not opened (HS53). Do not blend the follow-ups.
- **D2. How serious is a recluse bite?** UC IPM / UCR: about 10% / 90% split, most heal without intervention (HS54–HS55). CDC: venom can cause a severe necrotic lesion that will require professional medical attention (HS56). UKY ENTFACT-622: "The bite of a brown recluse is serious. It is associated with tissue destruction and may require surgical treatment." Use UC IPM's percentages plus CDC's "can cause" / "seek care." Do not flatten this into "it's nothing" or "it always rots."
- **D3. Jump length.** MDC gives "up to 40" / "up to 50" for some species, including *Colonus puerperus* (HS24). UC IPM only says "jumping distances many times their body length" (HS21). Australian Museum has no number (HS26).
- **D4. Can a cellar spider pierce skin?** Penn State: not known to bite people (HS13). UC IPM, about cellar spiders in California: "only marginally capable of biting humans because their fangs are too short to pierce people’s skin in most cases." Australian Museum: it had been thought fangs could not pierce human skin; a Mythbusters test showed tiny fangs (about 0.25 mm) of this species were capable, with a very short-lived sting. Zobel-Thropp 2019 documents mild human stings for *P. mexicanus*, not *P. phalangioides* (HS20). Do not say "they cannot bite you," and do not use "fangs too short" as if it proved the venom legend (M1).
- **D5. Which cellar spider is in the house?** Penn State: *P. phalangioides* is the most common pholcid in the United States. UC IPM: in California the introduced marbled cellar spider, *Holocnemus pluchei*, has since displaced the once common *P. phalangioides*. Label the species if California is on screen.
- **D6. Wolf-spider bites.** MDC: "harmless" if they bite when provoked (HS31). Penn State: will bite if mishandled; symptoms generally subside within 24 hours; no serious medical consequences noted (HS32). UKY: strong fangs, could bite if disturbed or pinched (HS33). Prefer Penn State's handling warning in any spoken bite line.
- **D7. First aid details.** Prefer CDC (HS63) and UC IPM (HS64): wash, cold/ice, elevate (CDC), do not attempt to remove venom, seek professional care. MedlinePlus adds 10-minutes-on / 10-off ice, a homemade splint, and "keep the affected area still … to prevent the venom from spreading." Do not promote those extras in the script.
- **D8. Black widow hourglass.** It can be imperfect or, on rare occasions, completely absent (HS8). Hourglass-only ID can fail.
- **D9. Giant house spider range.** The 4-inch span is a PNW *E. atrica* fact (HS34–HS36), not a generic house-spider size.
- **D10. False-widow "wasp sting."** UC IPM uses a yellowjacket-sting comparison for *Steatoda grossa* (HS40). NHM does not use that wording for the skull-backed noble false widow (HS41).
- **D11. Cellar body length.** Penn State: females 7–8 mm, males 6 mm (HS14). BAS factsheet: males 7–10 mm, females 8–10 mm. Do not blend.

## Myths and warnings

**Myths (do not say, even as an unsourced joke):**
- **M1.** "Daddy longlegs are the most venomous spiders in the world, but their fangs are too short to bite you." Bust: HS17–HS20.
- **M2.** "A daddy longlegs is a spider" as if the name meant one animal. Bust: HS19.
- **M3.** "The violin mark means it is a brown recluse." Bust: HS9, HS48.
- **M4.** "Brown recluses live all over the United States / in California." Bust: HS46, HS62.
- **M5.** "A nasty skin wound is a recluse bite" without the spider in hand, especially outside the range. Bust: HS57.
- **M6.** "False widows are as dangerous as black widows." Bust: HS39–HS41.
- **M7.** "The female black widow always eats the male." Bust: HS43, HS70.
- **M8.** "Every house spider should be killed." Bust: HS1, HS5, HS13, HS23, HS31, HS37.
- **M9.** "Jumping spiders jump 40 times their length" / "from your window," as if that were every jumper. Bust: HS24.

**Warnings — unsafe to imitate or unsafe to recommend:**
- **W1.** Do not poke a cellar spider (or any spider) with a finger to make it blur. BAS describes that as how they react, not as a thing to do.
- **W2.** Do not pick up, corner, or handle wolf spiders, and never handle a spider that might be a widow or a recluse. Wolf spiders bite if mishandled or trapped against skin (HS32). Recluses can bite when trapped against skin (HS10). Widows bite when disturbed in a retreat or when trapped.
- **W3.** Do not misuse labeled control products: no off-label mixes, no outdoor broadcast spraying as the first step, no treating a whole yard for spiders. Follow the label. Extension pages say such products are usually not needed and do not give long-term control without housekeeping (HS65). UKY recommendations are registered for Kentucky only.
- **W4.** Do not attempt to remove venom, cut a bite, or suck it. CDC: "Do not attempt to remove venom." Seek professional medical attention (HS63).
- **W5.** Jar-and-paper removal is for spiders you are leaving alone, not a dare to trap a widow or recluse with bare hands. Wear gloves when cleaning clutter and woodpiles (UC IPM). Shake out shoes and clothing in recluse country rather than reaching in blindly (HS58).
- **W6.** Do not mail specimens in alcohol (postal / DOT rules, HS60–HS61). Do not crush a spider you still need identified.
- **W7.** Do not use a blurry photo as a medical identification, and do not treat a wound as a recluse bite on a guess — that can delay care for infections such as MRSA or necrotizing fasciitis (HS57).
- **W8.** MedlinePlus homemade-splint and "keep still to stop venom spreading" lines should not be imitated from this pack; they are not on the CDC page used for spoken first aid (D7).
