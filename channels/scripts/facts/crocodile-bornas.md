# Fact pack: "Your Life as a Crocodile: 8 Things You Have to Get Through" (crocodile born-as long-form)

Prepared for the crocodile born-as long-form in `/workspace/drafts/longform-pipeline-pitch-1117.md`, concept 5 (script due Sat 11/21). Research date 2026-11-15 (ET). Row IDs CB1 to CB55. Format follows the dog pack: ID | fact | exact quote | URL | scope and how it was opened | do not say.

**Lead species: saltwater / estuarine crocodile, *Crocodylus porosus*.** Chosen because (1) the required bite-force beat is locked to one *C. porosus* in Erickson et al. 2012, PLoS ONE; (2) official Be Crocwise safety wording from Queensland, the Northern Territory and Parks Australia (Kakadu) is species-matched and detailed; (3) IUCN, the Australian Museum, Queensland's estuarine-crocodile page, NT government pages, and Perth Zoo all opened live today with species-specific nest, hatchling, maturity and hunting figures. Nile crocodile (*C. niloticus*) was not used as the lead: official African park safety pages were thinner in this session, and using Nile would force mixing the 16,414 N figure across species. Do not mix saltwater numbers with Nile, freshwater crocodile (*C. johnstoni*), alligator or "crocodilians in general" unless the row itself labels the other taxon.

Labels: [raw] = fetched by curl from the box today and quoted from the extracted text (copies in `.croc-src/`). [pdf] = official PDF (or IUCN's generated assessment PDF-HTML) downloaded today. [fetch] = opened live today through the fetch tool, because nt.gov.au served the box a challenge page; excerpt saved in `.croc-src/nt-crocs-webfetch.txt`. Date checked for every row: 2026-11-15.

## 0. Read this first
- Safety lines keep their official trigger and are said clean (CB41–CB51). The joke comes after, never inside the instruction. No advice to approach, feed, hold a jaw shut, swim, or outrun a crocodile.
- Bite-force figures keep the "one animal, one study, one species" condition (CB21). Never say "crocodiles bite at 3,689 pounds."
- Survival figures keep their measure and their hedge: share of eggs that hatch is not the same as share of hatchlings that reach one year, which is not the same as share that reach maturity (D6).
- No spoken citations (Social Studio rule). Each line carries its source in an on-screen tag; the description carries the list.
- No speed figure in the hunting or people beats, even though Parks Australia states some (M8). The brief bars a "can you outrun" number.
- Existing bite-force note in research 95 / the croc Short outline reused only after the PLoS ONE paper itself was reopened today. No crocodile born-as pack existed before this file.

## 3. Jokes cannot say / myths and warnings

Unsafe to imitate
- Do not approach, feed, interfere with, or "just look at" a crocodile, including a small one (CB46, CB49, CB51).
- Do not joke that someone held a crocodile's jaws shut, even though closing is much more powerful than opening (CB52). That stunt is unsafe and is not what the sources say to do.
- Do not give a "can you outrun it" race, a zigzag tip, or a speed as survival advice. Parks Australia busts the zigzag myth and does print some speeds (M8); still do not build a beat around racing one.
- Do not joke that Noodle, Meatball or a person swam, waded, crouched at the edge, retrieved a lure, or camped on the bank and was fine.
- No joke on the Kakadu "have attacked and killed people" line (CB48), or right after it.

Myths and common errors found this session
- M1. "10-metre saltwater crocodiles." NT Be Crocwise says "Crocodiles up to 10 metres have been recorded in the wild in the past, but are extremely rare." The Australian Museum's carefully measured record is "at least 615 cm," and NT.gov.au's own "biggest reported" line for the 1974 Mary River animal is 6.7 m. Do not use 10 metres.
- M2. "A 17-foot saltwater crocodile bit at 3,689 pounds." The existing Short outline used that length. Erickson et al. 2012 measured animals only up to 4.59 m (about 15.1 ft). The paper does not give the 16,414 N animal's length in the sentence that reports that force. 17 feet is longer than any crocodile in that study.
- M3. "3,700 psi." The Queensland Museum blog (15 April 2026) says the saltwater crocodile's bite reaches "around 3,700 pounds per square inch (psi)." That confuses the paper's 3,689 lbs of force with pressure. Use newtons or pounds of force, from the paper, with the one-animal hedge.
- M4. "Saltwater crocodiles only live in salt water." False. Queensland, NT, IUCN and Kakadu all say they also live in freshwater, sometimes hundreds of kilometres inland. Kakadu's myth-busting list leads with this.
- M5. "Crocodiles can't open their mouths underwater, so they can't attack underwater." Kakadu: false. They can open the mouth underwater to attack; they need the head above water, or to be on land, to swallow without taking in water.
- M6. "If you can see the bottom, it's safe to swim." Kakadu: false. "It's OK if you don't stay in too long" is also false: "it takes less than a second to be attacked."
- M7. "A fire makes it safe to camp beside a river." Kakadu: false. Camp 50 m from the water and at least 2 m above the high-water mark.
- M8. Speed myths, from the same Kakadu page (official, but not for an outrun beat): "Crocodiles can swim up to 60km/h" is false; "crocodiles can swim 10-15km/h on average." "You can outwit a crocodile by running from them in a zigzag pattern" is false; the page says to run in a straight line because they "can only manage short bursts of speed when coming out of the water." It also says "when charging from the water crocodiles have been measured moving at 12 metres per second." Do not put that number in the hunting or people beat, and do not turn it into a race. San Diego Zoo's general "swim up to 20 mph" / "run as fast as 11 mph" lines were not used; they are not species-specific and are not peer-reviewed.
- M9. "Unchanged for 65 million years" (Australia Zoo) and "a descendant from the dinosaur age" (NT.gov.au). Both over-simplify. Crocodilians are not descendants of dinosaurs. Do not say "living dinosaurs."
- M10. "27 species of crocodilian" (Australia Zoo). Erickson et al. 2012 tested the 23 extant species then recognised by IUCN-SSC. Do not use the zoo's 27 in the bite beat.
