What is life like for a saltwater crocodile? This is your whole life as one, from a seventy-gram hatchling in a vegetation mound to the waterline: 8 things you have to get through, then the verdict. Meatball tried all eight from the couch, with his dog, Noodle, as the stand-in and his cat, Sauce, as everybody else.

Checked facts in this video:
- Saltwater crocodile hatchlings are about 70 grams and about 30 centimetres long (Queensland Government).
- In Australian counts, females lay about 50 eggs, or 40 to 70. Incubation is typically 80 to 90 days, depending on temperature (IUCN Red List; Queensland Government).
- Nest temperature decides sex. On the Queensland page, 32°C produces both sexes but most are male; lower or higher temperatures produce more females; very high or very low temperatures kill the embryos (Queensland Government).
- The Australian Museum estimates that up to 75% of eggs laid in a season will not hatch, mostly from infertility, flooding, overheating, poor gas exchange and drying out, not predators.
- Northern Territory figures say, on average, only 25% of eggs will hatch and 54% of hatchlings will survive the first year. Only 1% of hatchlings are thought to survive to maturity in the wild (NT Government). The page is official but names no study and no count.
- In one study, one saltwater crocodile produced a bite force of 16,414 N (about 3,689 lb), the highest measured in any animal. Seven adult saltwater crocodiles were tested; the representative value for the species was 8,983 N (Erickson et al. 2012, PLoS ONE). Never read this as 'crocodiles bite at 3,689 pounds.'
- Estuarine crocodiles wait in ambush at the water's edge. They can attack in knee-deep water, so even wading can be dangerous (Queensland Government; Parks Australia, Kakadu). Large saltwater crocodiles can stay underwater for up to one hour (NT Government).
- Females usually mature at about 12 years; males in the mid-teens, depending on the official page (Queensland Government; NT Be Crocwise). Average adult length is 3–5 m (Australian Museum). The largest reliably measured animal, Mary River, 1974, was at least 615 cm (Australian Museum).
- IUCN lists Crocodylus porosus as Least Concern, with a stable trend. Queensland lists it as Vulnerable under state law. Those are different listings.
- Although they are called saltwater crocodiles, they also live in freshwater. They can travel hundreds of kilometres in the water, especially after heavy rain and floods. No waterway within their natural range can be considered crocodile-free (Queensland Be Crocwise).
- Safety, in areas where crocodiles live: keep out of the water; stay more than 5 metres away from the water's edge; be extra cautious at night, dusk and dawn (Queensland Be Crocwise). Never approach, interfere with or feed crocodiles, even small ones. Camp at least 50 metres from the water's edge. Any body of water in the Top End may contain large and potentially dangerous crocodiles (NT Be Crocwise). Saltwater crocodiles are dangerous. They have attacked and killed people. If there are no warning signs, assume crocodiles are present (Parks Australia, Kakadu). They can attack in knee-deep water, so even wading can be dangerous (Parks Australia, Kakadu).

General information, not safety advice for a specific place. Follow local warning signs and official Be Crocwise guidance.
