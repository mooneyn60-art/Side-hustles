## Chapter 1: how a tick gets on you

- L4: First, the thing everybody gets wrong. The CDC says ticks can't fly or jump. [T1]
- L5: So how do they get on you? Many species wait in a position called questing. [T1]
- L6: While questing, ticks hold onto leaves and grass by their third and fourth pair of legs. [T2]
- L7: Meatball asked if that is just waiting for a bus. Noodle did not answer. [J]
- L8: They find a host by detecting breath and body odors, or by sensing body heat, moisture and vibrations. [T3]
- L9: And when a host brushes the spot where a tick is waiting, it quickly climbs aboard. [T4]
- L10: Meatball is a dog. He brushes against everything. Hell of a design flaw. [J, S]

## Chapter 2: where in the yard

- L11: The CDC says ticks live in grassy, brushy or wooded areas, or even on animals. [T6]
- L13: On a lawn, fewer ticks are found in the sunny, manicured middle. [T13]
- L14: Meatball picked the sunny middle because of a sunbeam. Lucky accident. [J]
- L15: The risky strip is the edge, next to woods, stone walls or ornamental plantings. [T11]

## Chapter 3: the season people forget

- L18: Most people who get Lyme disease in Connecticut get it from the nymph stage of the deer tick, which is active in late spring and summer. [T15]
- L20: But adult deer ticks are active in the fall, on warm days of winter, and in spring. That is Connecticut; ask your local Extension office about your area. [T15]

## Re-hook (midpoint)

- L21: So what can you actually do about the yard? Meatball tried three things. Only the boring one worked. [R, J]

## Chapter 4: the yard

- L22: The CDC says to clear tall grasses and brush around homes and at the edge of lawns, and to remove leaf litter. [T17]
- L23: It also says to place a three foot wide barrier of wood chips or gravel between lawns and wooded areas. [T18]
- L26: And the CDC says you should not rely on spraying to reduce your risk of infection. [T21]

## Chapter 5: you, after the yard

- L27: Here is the part that matters most. Check your body, clothing, gear and pets for ticks during and after being outdoors, even in your own backyard. [T9]
- L28: If you have been in tall grass, brush or leaf litter, do that tick check when you come inside. [T7, T9]
- L30: The CDC says showering within two hours of coming indoors has been shown to reduce your risk of getting Lyme disease. [T8]
- L32: Meatball showered. Wet dog, still a dog. Nobody learned anything. [J]

## Chapter 6: the dog part

- L33: The CDC says pets can transport ticks from outside to inside, where the ticks may then bite people. Check pets daily for ticks. [T22]
- L35: Dogs and cats can get Lyme disease, but there is no evidence they spread it directly to their owners. [T23]
- L36: Ask your vet about tick prevention for your dog. The CDC says to talk to your veterinarian about the tickborne diseases in your area. [T24]
- L37: And for cats: ask your veterinarian before using any tick prevention product. [T24]

## Chapter 7: if one is attached

- L38: The CDC says that in most cases a tick must be attached for more than 24 hours before the Lyme disease bacterium can be transmitted. [T25]
- L39: And if you remove a tick within 24 hours, you can greatly reduce your chances of getting Lyme disease. [T25]
- L40: Use fine-tipped tweezers, grasp the tick as close to the skin's surface as possible, and pull with steady, even pressure. Do not twist or jerk. [T26]
- L41: Do not use petroleum jelly, heat, nail polish or other substances to make it let go. [T26]
- L42: If you get a rash or fever within several weeks, see your doctor. [T26]
