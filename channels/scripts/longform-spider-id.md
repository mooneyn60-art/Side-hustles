## Description

```
Most house spiders can be left alone if you can tell them apart. This is a house-spider ID guide: the ones you can leave, the ones you should not guess from a photo, and how to get a spider identified. Meatball sorts them from the couch, with his dog, Noodle, as the stand-in and his cat, Sauce, as everybody else.

Checked facts in this video:
- One Kansas home, a 19th-century house in Lenexa, had a family of four collect 2,055 brown recluse spiders in 6 months (UC IPM; UC Riverside, Rick Vetter, both citing Vetter & Barger 2002). The two pages disagree on the bite follow-up, so this video keeps them separate and does not blend them: UC IPM says they lived there nearly a decade, and only once in 10 years had anyone shown evidence of a bite, to a finger which turned red for a few days and then healed without incident. The UCR author page says about 450 of the spiders were large enough to cause envenomations, and that as of September 2004 no person or their pets had shown evidence of a bite in 8 years of occupancy. The journal PDF itself was not opened (HS53), so the paper is not quoted directly.
- The jaws of most spiders are too small to bite humans. On the same UC IPM page, the fangs of most spiders cannot penetrate the skin of an adult person.
- Venomous spiders found in the United States include the black widow and the brown recluse. Spiders are usually not aggressive, and most bites occur because a spider is trapped or touched (CDC / NIOSH). This does not mean only two spiders have venom.
- Longbodied cellar spiders (Pholcus phalangioides) are not known to bite people and are not a medically important species (Penn State). In one Hampshire garden shed, May to September, 63 of 102 prey remains in their webs were the common house spider (British Arachnological Society). That is one UK shed, one season. They have been recorded wrapping heavier large house spiders from a safe distance. This species presents absolutely no danger to humans or to pets. When threatened they rapidly vibrate in a circular fashion in the web. Do not poke a spider to make it blur.
- The name daddy longlegs is used for three different animals: cellar spiders, harvestmen, and crane flies. Only the spider builds a silk web and regularly lives in houses. Harvestmen have no venom of any kind (British Arachnological Society; Burke Museum). There is no scientific evidence that this spider has the most toxic venom of all spiders (Australian Museum). Pholcid venom is not unusually strong (Burke Museum). A 2019 venom study (Zobel-Thropp et al.) analysed male Physocyclus mexicanus, not the common longbodied cellar spider, and is not used as a fact about the spider in the corner.
- Some jumping spiders, including a colorful jumper common in grassy areas throughout Missouri (Colonus puerperus), can leap horizontally up to 40 times their length (Missouri Conservationist, October 2024). That is not a figure for every jumping spider, and it is not sourced as a jump from your window. Jumps from a slightly elevated position can stretch up to 50 body lengths; that is a different figure and is not spoken in the video. They make no capture web. They rarely bite people, and when they do it usually results in nothing more than a mild sting or an itchy welt.
- Wolf spiders do not spin a web to catch prey; they run it down. A female attaches the egg sac to her spinnerets, then later carries the babies on her back. They will bite if mishandled or trapped next to the skin. Typical reactions include initial pain and redness, with the potential for some localized swelling. Symptoms generally subside within 24 hours. No serious medical consequences of these bites have been noted (Penn State). Do not pick one up.
- The giant house spider (Eratigena atrica) is commonly found in and around Washington homes west of the Cascade Mountains, plus parts of coastal Canada and Oregon, and is not found east of the Cascades in Washington (WSU). For large adults, the leg span can reach up to 4 inches; body length is typically around 1/2 inch to 1 inch, not including the legs. The bite is not known to pose any threat to humans. If you are bitten by a spider, bring the spider to an expert for correct identification, and you may wish to seek medical attention. Giant house spiders are considered beneficial and should be left alone as much as reasonably possible. Indoors they are usually on the floor, or trapped in sinks or bathtubs.
- A mature female western black widow is about 1/2 inch, shiny jet black, with a red or orange hourglass on the underside. The hourglass can vary and on rare occasions is completely absent. She is the spider posing the greatest potential envenomation threat to humans in the western United States. Only the larger immature female and the adult female are able to bite through a person's skin and inject enough venom to cause a painful reaction. The male's fangs are too small to break human skin. Bites are quite rare, even where these spiders are very common. If bitten, seek medical attention immediately. Symptoms most often merely resemble those of the flu; black widow bites don't cause conspicuous swelling, necrosis, or deterioration of tissue around the bite (UC IPM).
- The false black widow (Steatoda grossa) is chocolate brown and never has red coloration on its belly; it is slightly smaller, about 3/8 inch, and unlike widow spiders it thrives indoors. Because they are common in homes, they are more frequently involved in bite incidents than black widow or brown widow spiders. They have mild venom, and bites may cause symptoms similar to those of yellowjacket wasp stings. That yellowjacket comparison is for this species only, not for the noble false widow, and is not spoken in the video.
- The noble false widow (Steatoda nobilis) usually has distinctive skull-shaped markings on its back. It looks similar to the more dangerous black widows, but is likely to give only a small and relatively harmless bite, and is only known to bite when provoked or trapped against skin (Natural History Museum). Usual symptom is pain lasting between 1 and 12 hours, rarely more than 24 hours. This video does not say the bite is like a wasp sting.
- The brown widow is a widow, not a false widow. The bite is much milder than that of the western black widow. Both black widows and brown widows are almost never found in homes, though they are common around them. The brown widow's egg sac is distinctive, with silk protuberances, resembling a very large pollen grain (spoken cut; kept here).
- The most definitive physical feature of recluse spiders is 6 equal-sized eyes arranged in 3 pairs. Most spiders have 8 eyes. The presence or absence of the violin marking is not a reliable way to identify this spider (UC IPM; UCR). If the spider has a body length greater than 1/2 inch, it is not a recluse. A brown recluse is about 3/8 inch in body length. It lives in the south-central Midwest, from southeastern Nebraska to southwestern Ohio and south through Texas to north Georgia. There are no populations of the brown recluse in California. If you do not live in the area on the range map, it is highly unlikely you have a recluse; possible, but incredibly unlikely.
- About 10% of brown recluse bites cause moderate or greater tissue damage and scarring, but the vast majority only result in inflammation and heal well without medical intervention. Deaths have been reported in children, but these are extremely rare. If a bite is suspected, still seek care (UC IPM). The venom can cause a severe lesion by destroying skin tissue; that lesion will require professional medical attention (CDC / NIOSH). A related UCR page puts the same split as 90% not medically significant; that is not a second study and is not a reason to skip care (spoken cut; kept here).
- Necrotic wounds are commonly misdiagnosed as brown recluse bites. In one national study of 11 university-based medical facilities, 29% of patients presenting for 'spider bite' had MRSA as the cause. Group A Streptococcus infection, which can cause necrotizing fasciitis, is sometimes misdiagnosed as a brown recluse bite. UC IPM says that infection has a fatality rate that can vary from 20 to 80% depending on how quickly it is correctly diagnosed. (Description only, not spoken.) Get a real diagnosis. Do not treat a wound as a recluse bite on a guess.
- Take a clear photo with a size reference, save the spider if you can do it safely, and take it to your county Cooperative Extension office. Use a ruler or coin next to the specimen. Never mail a spider in alcohol; that is a violation of postal regulations (UC IPM; University of Kentucky).
- If a spider bites you: stay calm; identify the type of spider if it is possible to do so safely; wash the bite area with soap and water; apply a cloth dampened with cold water or filled with ice; elevate the bite area if possible; do not attempt to remove venom; seek professional medical attention (CDC / NIOSH). If the bite of any spider causes an unusual or severe reaction, contact a physician, hospital, or poison control center. In the United States, Poison Help is 1-800-222-1222 (UC IPM).
- In the midwestern and southern United States, recluse spiders can be found indoors in dark closets, shoes, or attics. Inspect or shake out any clothing, shoes, towels, or equipment before use (CDC / NIOSH).
- Spiders are mostly beneficial because they feed on pest insects. Pesticides are not generally needed. Insecticides will not provide long-term control and should not generally be used against spiders outdoors. Always read and follow label directions (UC IPM).
- Symptoms associated with spider bites can vary from minor to severe. Although extremely rare, death can occur in the most severe cases (CDC / NIOSH).

General information, not medical advice for a specific bite or a specific house. If a bite is suspected, seek professional medical care. Follow your county Cooperative Extension office and official public-health guidance.
```
