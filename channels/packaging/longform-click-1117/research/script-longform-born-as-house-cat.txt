You were just born as a house cat. Your eyes are shut. They open in about a week to ten days. You probably have a few siblings. Litters average about four. And the deal for your whole life was made thousands of years before you got here. Eight things come with that deal. Here they are, in the order they hit you. Then we add it up. Raw deal, or sweet deal? Number one. The deal itself. A 2007 genetic study of 979 cats and their wild cousins traced house cats to the Near East. Probably around the time the first farming villages appeared. Scientists think your ancestors showed up for the mice in the grain stores. And they probably just moved themselves in. A 2007 paper cites cat and human remains found together on Cyprus, about 9,500 years old. And you still look a lot like them. A feral cat with the wild tabby pattern is hard to tell from a wildcat. So you're basically a wildcat. With a litter box. And a clock that is already running. Number two. You get about five weeks to learn humans. Merck's vet manual calls it the socialization period: about two to seven weeks old. Kittens handled daily by people in their first month have been found to be more outgoing and more social with humans. Nobody tells you the clock is running. You're three weeks old. Your opinion of humans may be shaped by a guy who holds you like a damn sandwich. Number three. Your senses were built for a different job. You most likely can't taste sugar. One of the two genes for the sweet receptor is broken. Birthday cake? Probably wasted on you. Hell of a start. You see fewer colours than your human. Scientists still argue about how many. And your lens seems to change shape only a little, so focusing up close is limited. But here's what you got instead. In dim light, you see about six times better than a person. Not in the dark. That part is a myth. You still need a little light. And in a classic test with two cats, hearing ran from 48 hertz all the way up to 85 kilohertz. One of the widest ranges of any mammal. Badass. Shame about the menu. Number four. Your menu. Your wild ancestors were obligate carnivores. Their prey had almost no carbs in it. And your body makes very little taurine by itself. It has to come from your food. In 1987, vets wrote that thousands of pet cats were dying every year of a heart disease, and nobody knew the cause. They found low taurine in 21 cats with failing hearts. Cats fed commercial cat food. They gave the cats taurine. After that, their heart function went back to normal. So some food back then was probably breaking hearts. Actual hearts. Damn. Earlier, in a 1975 lab study, cats on a diet with casein as the protein had the light-sensing cells in their eyes break down. Low taurine was the suspect there too. And that saucer of milk from every cartoon? The ASPCA says milk can give pets diarrhea. Meatball brought milk. Meatball may regret it. And that saucer is the least of your problems. Number five. The house itself. Start with the flowers. The FDA says true lilies and daylilies can cause fatal kidney failure in cats. A bite of leaf or petal, a few pollen grains licked off fur, or water from the vase can do it. Fatal kidney failure, in under three days. The FDA says this toxin only affects cats, and nobody has identified it. If treatment waits eighteen hours or more, the kidney damage is generally permanent. So if you might have touched one, your human calls a vet, right away. Peace lilies and calla lilies are a different, milder problem. Next, the medicine cabinet. The ASPCA's poison experts say one extra-strength dose of acetaminophen can kill a cat if treatment doesn't start soon. If you get into any, your human calls a vet or a poison line right away. String and thread can block your gut and may need surgery. And then there's the window. One vet school counted 119 cats brought in after falling from buildings. 109 of them lived. Four died. Six were put down, four of those because of the cost of treatment. Nearly half had broken limbs. And that only counts the cats that made it to the vet. So always lands on its feet needs an asterisk. Vets who studied 1,125 falls in Berlin say window barriers can help. That's five. So far, the damn furniture is winning. And we haven't met the people with good intentions yet. Number six. The carrier. Vet guidelines say you should be examined at least once a year, and at least every six months once you're a senior. But owners say the carrier, the drive, and a scared cat at the clinic are reasons they go less. Fair. Have you seen the carrier? A dungeon with a handle. Go anyway. In one British study, about one in five cats hadn't seen a vet in the year before their eighth birthday. Then there's the surgery. Getting fixed is one of the most common vet operations in the United States. In a small 1997 study, six neutered males gained about 30 percent in weight. Five intact males gained about 12. But in British vet records, being neutered went with a longer life. That's a link, not proof. Still: neutering can mean weight gain, and maybe more years. Hell of a trade. And declawing. It's surgery, not a nail trim. New York banned it in 2019, except for medical reasons. The law names three surgeries: declawing, phalangectomy and tendonectomy. And studies are mixed on whether the pain lasts. And all of that comes from the people who love you. Number seven. Your human. He means well. He pets you. In two studies, cats reacted worst to being stroked near the tail. In one of them, the best-rated spot was between the eyes and the ears. He goes straight for the tail end. Every damn time. Here's the rude part. In the 2015 study, cats showed more negative behaviour with a person they knew than with a stranger. The authors say it needs more research. He calls your name. You can probably tell it from other words. In that test, over half of the cats just moved their ears or heads, name or not. Fewer than one in ten let out a meow. But you get one win. The purr. A 2009 study found that some cats, when they want food, add a high, cry-like sound to the purr. People rated those purrs as more urgent and less pleasant. Even people who had never owned a cat. Take the cry out, and the urgency ratings dropped. The university's news release says cats appear to be exploiting the human instinct to care for babies. You may have hacked your damn human. Before breakfast. Enjoy it, because your body has plans. Number eight. Your own body. The vets who write the pain guidelines say pain in cats has traditionally been under-recognised. The signs can be subtle. Here's what X-rays found. Researchers X-rayed 100 cats of all ages from one practice. On X-ray, 92 of them had signs of joint disease. That's X-ray signs. It doesn't tell us how many were hurting. Kidneys. The number depends on who you test and how. 11% 77% About 11 percent of older cats screened at two British clinics. Of the cats with kidney disease, three in four owners had seen no change in their peeing. And all 129 senior cats in the study had a dental problem. Every damn one. Hairballs, at least, are honest. A Cornell vet says one every week or two is nothing to worry about. Unless you get tired, stop eating for a day or two, or keep retching with nothing coming up. Then it's a vet, without delay. So. Eight for eight. Raw as hell? Before you answer, look at what's waiting on the other side of the door. Researchers followed more than 2,400 British pet cats for eight years. The study didn't record which ones went outside. Of the 362 cats that died, 165 died in road accidents. Almost half. For kittens under one, it was 41 of 67 deaths. 61 percent. In one study of managed feral colonies, three in four kittens died or vanished before six months. Those are not pet-cat odds. And the ASPCA estimates that about three million cats entered US shelters and rescues in 2025. About 277,000 were euthanized. The British researchers put it plainly. Keeping cats indoors can cut the road risk. But it may make a cat's behavioural needs harder to meet. So here's the ledger. In British vet records, a newborn kitten can expect about eleven and three quarter years. In a second study of English cats that died, half made it past fourteen. That first table misses cats that died before a vet visit, which flatters it. Most deaths in it were euthanasia, which drags it down. Read it as a sketch. Guinness says the oldest cat ever lived 38 years and 3 days. Tough old badass. And in that eight-year study of 2,400 British pet cats, the recorded chance of dying in any one year, up to age eight, was under four percent. So a safe house could win on years. What it owes you is everything else. The cat vets' own guidelines call meeting your environmental needs essential, not optional. A safe place. Your food, water, litter and scratching spots, kept apart. Play that lets you hunt. Humans who are friendly and predictable. And a home that respects your nose. Get those, plus a safe house and vet visits, and the deal looks pretty damn sweet. Miss them, and it's a lot rawer. Free rent, though.
