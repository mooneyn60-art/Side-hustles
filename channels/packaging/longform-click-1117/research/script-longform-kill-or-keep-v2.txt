One bug in your house can put you in the hospital. One protects you, and you squish it anyway. First, the shocker. The bug you squish most is the one protecting you. The house centipede. It comes out at night, and hunts the bugs you actually hate. Cockroach babies. Bed bugs. Silverfish. Carpet beetle larvae. It came from around the Mediterranean, and was first found in Pennsylvania in 1849. Grab it by a leg, and the leg pops right off. It's built to break. Can it sting you? Only if you handle it roughly. Experts say that's rare, and not dangerous. Verdict: keep it. It's free pest control, working the night shift. Number two. The skinny spider hanging in your basement corner. The cellar spider. Poke its web, and it shakes so fast it turns into a blur. It builds loose, messy webs in dark, damp corners. It's one of the top hunters of other spiders in your house. And a mom carries her eggs in her jaws, everywhere she goes. That old story about deadly venom? Spider experts found no evidence it's dangerous to people. Verdict: keep it. Just sweep the old webs if they bother you. The fuzzy jumping spider on your windowsill. Big front eyes, and no web at all. It stalks a bug, then pounces. Some can leap up to forty times their own body length. It hunts in the daytime, on your plants and across the floor. At night it hangs from a silk thread, and scientists caught its eyes twitching, in a sleep-like state. Bite you? Most spiders can't harm people, and its bite is usually not serious. Verdict: keep it. It eats pest insects, for free. Now, the kill pile. The carpet beetle. Round, and spotted white, brown and yellow. It's drawn to light, so you find the adults on your windows. The real damage is the babies, hiding in your closet. Wool, fur, feathers, silk. The giveaway? Their little shed skins, under furniture and along the carpet. Museums use their cousins to clean skeletons, down to the tiniest bones. Verdict: kill it. Vacuum the carpet edges, the closets, and under the couch. The next one drinks blood, and hides where you sleep. Bed bugs. Their eggs are about the size of a speck of dust. And every baby needs a blood meal before it can grow. Five times. They ride home in your luggage, your bags, and your folded clothes. In the cold, they can wait up to a year between meals. The CDC says they're not known to spread disease. But the bites itch. Verdict: kill it, fast. One female can lay hundreds of eggs in her life. The next three can actually make you sick. The cockroach. One egg case can hatch up to forty babies. And one female can turn into thousands of roaches in under a year. Step on it? It can survive nine hundred times its own weight pressing down. It can even live for weeks without its head. The real danger is what it leaves behind. Droppings and shed skins can trigger asthma, especially in kids. Verdict: kill it. Then take away the crumbs and the water it lives on. This one gets more people than you'd think. The house mouse. It doesn't even need to drink. It can get its water from your food. It chews through wires, gas lines and appliance cords. A female can have up to ten litters a year. It rarely goes more than thirty feet from its nest. So if you see one, the nest is close. And mice can carry diseases, like salmonella and leptospirosis. Found droppings? Don't sweep them dry. The CDC says soak them with disinfectant first, with gloves on. Verdict: kill it, or trap it. Then seal every gap a quarter inch or wider. The last two can put you in the hospital. The black widow. It hangs upside down in a messy, sticky web, so the red hourglass faces out. Its venom hits your nerves. A bad bite can mean cramps that spread through your whole body, and heavy sweating. One female can make up to nine egg sacs in a summer. And the famous part? She rarely eats her mate. Only if she's really hungry. A death is very unlikely. But if one bites you, see a doctor. Verdict: danger. Wear gloves around woodpiles and garage clutter. And the one I promised. The brown recluse. It hunts at night. By dawn, it can crawl into your shoes, or the clothes on your floor. About one bite in ten causes real skin damage, and a scar. Most bites heal well on their own, and deaths are extremely rare. Still, a bad bite needs a doctor. It lives in the south-central U.S. Outside that range, you very likely don't have one. Striped legs, or spiny legs? Then it's not a recluse. Verdict: danger. Shake out your shoes, and pull your bed away from the wall. So, which one is living in your house? Tell me in the comments.
